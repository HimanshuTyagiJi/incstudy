Q1. Prove that the given matrix $A$ is Hermitian ($A^\theta = A$):

🔥 Repeated in 2017 & 2018 3 Marks

Prove that $(\overline{A})^T = A$ for each of the following matrices:

Part (a) · Dec 2018 ($2 \times 2$ Matrix) $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1+2i \\ 1-2i & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$
Part (b) · Dec 2017 ($3 \times 3$ Matrix) $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1-i & 2 \\ 1+i & 3 & i \\ 2 & -i & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Definition: A square matrix $A$ is called a Hermitian Matrix if its conjugate transpose equals the matrix itself, i.e., $A^\theta = (\overline{A})^T = A$.

Part (a) Solution ($2 \times 2$ Matrix):

Given matrix $A$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1+2i \\ 1-2i & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Take complex conjugate $\overline{A}$ (replace $i$ with $-i$):

$$\overline{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1-2i \\ 1+2i & 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

2. Take transpose $(\overline{A})^T$ (interchange rows and columns):

$$A^\theta = (\overline{A})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1+2i \\ 1-2i & 2 \end{bmatrix} = A$$

Since $A^\theta = A$, matrix $A$ is Hermitian. Hence proved.

Part (b) Solution ($3 \times 3$ Matrix):

Given matrix $A$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1-i & 2 \\ 1+i & 3 & i \\ 2 & -i & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

1. Take complex conjugate $\overline{A}$:

$$\overline{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1+i & 2 \\ 1-i & 3 & -i \\ 2 & i & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

2. Take transpose $(\overline{A})^T$:

$$A^\theta = (\overline{A})^T = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1-i & 2 \\ 1+i & 3 & i \\ 2 & -i & 0 \end{bmatrix} = A$$

Since $A^\theta = A$, matrix $A$ is Hermitian. Hence proved.

Q2. Show that $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{|x - 2|}{x - 2}$ does not exist.

🔥 Repeated in 2015, 2018, 2019 (3 Times) 3 Marks

Evaluate the Left Hand Limit (LHL) and Right Hand Limit (RHL) as $x \to 2$ to show that they are not equal.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

By definition of absolute value function:

$$|x - 2| = \begin{cases} +(x - 2), & \text{when } x \gt 2 \\ -(x - 2), & \text{when } x \lt 2 \end{cases}$$

1. Left Hand Limit (LHL) at $x = 2$:

Substitute $x = 2 - h$, where $h > 0$ and $h \to 0$:

$$\text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to 2^-} \frac{|x - 2|}{x - 2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|(2 - h) - 2|}{(2 - h) - 2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|-h|}{-h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{-h} = -1$$

2. Right Hand Limit (RHL) at $x = 2$:

Substitute $x = 2 + h$, where $h > 0$ and $h \to 0$:

$$\text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 2^+} \frac{|x - 2|}{x - 2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|(2 + h) - 2|}{(2 + h) - 2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h} = +1$$

Conclusion:

Since $\text{LHL} \neq \text{RHL}$ ($-1 \neq +1$), the limit does not exist:

$$\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{|x - 2|}{x - 2} \quad \text{does not exist. Hence proved.}$$

Q3. Define rank of a matrix with an example.

Repeated in 2016 & 2019 3 Marks

State the definition of matrix rank in terms of non-zero minors and linearly independent row vectors.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Definition: The rank of a matrix $A$, denoted by $\rho(A)$, is defined as the maximum order of any non-zero minor of matrix $A$.

A matrix $A$ is of rank $r$ if:

  1. There exists at least one non-zero minor of order $r$.
  2. Every minor of order $(r + 1)$ and higher (if any exists) is equal to zero.

Example:

Consider the matrix $A$ of order $2 \times 3$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 2 & 4 & 6 \end{bmatrix}$$

All possible $2 \times 2$ minors are:

$$\begin{vmatrix} 1 & 2 \\ 2 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 4 - 4 = 0, \quad \begin{vmatrix} 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 6 \end{vmatrix} = 12 - 12 = 0, \quad \begin{vmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 2 & 6 \end{vmatrix} = 6 - 6 = 0$$

Since all order 2 minors are zero, but there are non-zero order 1 minors (e.g. $|1| = 1 \neq 0$):

$$\text{Rank}(A) = \rho(A) = 1$$

Q4. Define Beta and Gamma functions and write their relation formula.

🔥 Repeated in 2015 & 2017 3 Marks

State the definite integral formulas for $\beta(m, n)$ and $\Gamma(n)$, and give their fundamental relationship: $$\beta(m, n) = \frac{\Gamma(m)\,\Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m+n)}$$

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

1. Beta Function $\beta(m, n)$ (First Eulerian Integral):

For $m > 0$ and $n > 0$, the Beta function is defined by the definite integral:

$$\beta(m, n) = \int_0^1 x^{m-1} (1 - x)^{n-1} \, dx$$

Symmetry: $\beta(m, n) = \beta(n, m)$.

2. Gamma Function $\Gamma(n)$ (Second Eulerian Integral):

For $n > 0$, the Gamma function is defined by the improper integral:

$$\Gamma(n) = \int_0^\infty e^{-x} x^{n-1} \, dx$$

Key values: $\Gamma(n+1) = n\,\Gamma(n) = n!$ (for integers $n \ge 0$) and $\Gamma\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) = \sqrt{\pi}$.

3. Fundamental Relation Formula:

$$\beta(m, n) = \frac{\Gamma(m) \, \Gamma(n)}{\Gamma(m + n)}$$

Q5. Show that $A = \begin{bmatrix} ab & b^2 \\ -a^2 & -ab \end{bmatrix}$ is a nilpotent matrix of order 2.

Dec 2016 3 Marks

Compute the matrix product $A^2 = A \cdot A$ and verify that $A^2 = O$.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Definition: A square matrix $A$ is nilpotent of order 2 if $A^2 = O$ (null matrix) and $A \neq O$.

Given matrix $A$:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} ab & b^2 \\ -a^2 & -ab \end{bmatrix}$$

Compute $A^2 = A \cdot A$:

$$A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} ab & b^2 \\ -a^2 & -ab \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} ab & b^2 \\ -a^2 & -ab \end{bmatrix}$$

Multiplying rows by columns:

  • Row 1 $\times$ Col 1: $(ab)(ab) + (b^2)(-a^2) = a^2 b^2 - a^2 b^2 = 0$
  • Row 1 $\times$ Col 2: $(ab)(b^2) + (b^2)(-ab) = ab^3 - ab^3 = 0$
  • Row 2 $\times$ Col 1: $(-a^2)(ab) + (-ab)(-a^2) = -a^3 b + a^3 b = 0$
  • Row 2 $\times$ Col 2: $(-a^2)(b^2) + (-ab)(-ab) = -a^2 b^2 + a^2 b^2 = 0$
$$A^2 = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = O$$

Since $A^2 = O$, matrix $A$ is a nilpotent matrix of order 2. Hence proved.

Q7. Differentiate the variable-power functions with respect to $x$:

🔥 Repeated in 2015 & 2019 3 Marks

Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ using logarithmic differentiation for each case:

Part (a) · Nov 2019 $$y = (\sin x)^x$$
Part (b) · Dec 2015 $$y = x^{x^2}$$
✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Part (a) · Nov 2019: $y = (\sin x)^x$

Taking natural logarithm ($\log$) on both sides:

$$\log y = \log\left[(\sin x)^x\right] = x \log(\sin x)$$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using the Product Rule:

$$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}(x) \cdot \log(\sin x) + x \cdot \frac{d}{dx}[\log(\sin x)]$$ $$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = 1 \cdot \log(\sin x) + x \cdot \left(\frac{1}{\sin x} \cdot \cos x\right)$$ $$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = \log(\sin x) + x \cot x$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx} = y \left[\log(\sin x) + x \cot x\right]$$

Substituting $y = (\sin x)^x$:

$$\mathbf{\frac{dy}{dx} = (\sin x)^x \left[\log(\sin x) + x \cot x\right]}$$

Part (b) · Dec 2015: $y = x^{x^2}$

Taking natural logarithm ($\log$) on both sides:

$$\log y = \log\left(x^{x^2}\right) = x^2 \log x$$

Differentiating both sides with respect to $x$ using the Product Rule:

$$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}(x^2) \cdot \log x + x^2 \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(\log x)$$ $$\frac{1}{y} \frac{dy}{dx} = 2x \log x + x^2 \cdot \frac{1}{x} = 2x \log x + x = x(2\log x + 1)$$ $$\frac{dy}{dx} = y \cdot x(2\log x + 1)$$

Substituting $y = x^{x^2}$:

$$\mathbf{\frac{dy}{dx} = x^{x^2} \cdot x(2\log x + 1) = x^{x^2 + 1} (2\log x + 1)}$$

Q8. Verify Rolle's theorem for $f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 4x - 2$ on $[-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}]$.

Nov 2019 3 Marks

Verify that $f(x)$ is continuous on $[-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}]$, differentiable on $(-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$, satisfies $f(-\sqrt{2}) = f(\sqrt{2})$, and find $c \in (-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$ such that $f'(c) = 0$.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Given polynomial: $f(x) = 2x^3 + x^2 - 4x - 2$ on $[-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}]$.

Step 1: Check the 3 Conditions of Rolle's Theorem:

  1. Continuity: Being a polynomial function, $f(x)$ is continuous on $[-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}]$.
  2. Differentiability: Derivative $f'(x) = 6x^2 + 2x - 4$ exists for all $x \in (-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$, so $f(x)$ is differentiable on $(-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$.
  3. Equal Endpoint Values: $$f(-\sqrt{2}) = 2(-\sqrt{2})^3 + (-\sqrt{2})^2 - 4(-\sqrt{2}) - 2 = -4\sqrt{2} + 2 + 4\sqrt{2} - 2 = 0$$ $$f(\sqrt{2}) = 2(\sqrt{2})^3 + (\sqrt{2})^2 - 4(\sqrt{2}) - 2 = 4\sqrt{2} + 2 - 4\sqrt{2} - 2 = 0$$ $$\implies f(-\sqrt{2}) = f(\sqrt{2}) = 0$$

Step 2: Find $c \in (-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2})$ such that $f'(c) = 0$:

$$f'(c) = 6c^2 + 2c - 4 = 0$$

Dividing by 2:

$$3c^2 + c - 2 = 0 \implies 3c^2 + 3c - 2c - 2 = 0$$ $$(3c - 2)(c + 1) = 0 \implies c = \frac{2}{3} \quad \text{or} \quad c = -1$$

Conclusion:

Both $c = -1$ and $c = \frac{2}{3} \approx 0.67$ lie strictly within the open interval $(-\sqrt{2}, \sqrt{2}) \approx (-1.414, 1.414)$. Hence, Rolle's theorem is verified.

Q9. Show that the parabola $y^2 - 4ax = 0$ has no asymptote.

Dec 2017 3 Marks

Examine the highest degree terms to prove that no parallel or oblique asymptotes exist for $y^2 = 4ax$.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Given curve equation: $y^2 - 4ax = 0$ (Degree 2 curve).

1. Asymptotes Parallel to Axes:

  • Parallel to y-axis: The highest power of $y$ is $y^2$, and its coefficient is $1$ (a non-zero constant). Equating to zero yields $1 = 0$, which is impossible. Hence, no asymptote parallel to y-axis.
  • Parallel to x-axis: The highest power of $x$ is $x^1$, and its coefficient is $-4a$. Equating to zero yields $-4a = 0$, which is impossible since $a \neq 0$ for a real parabola. Hence, no asymptote parallel to x-axis.

2. Oblique Asymptotes ($y = mx + c$):

Substitute $y = mx + c$ into $y^2 - 4ax = 0$:

$$(mx + c)^2 - 4ax = 0 \implies m^2 x^2 + (2mc - 4a)x + c^2 = 0$$

For an oblique asymptote, the coefficients of the two highest powers of $x$ must vanish simultaneously:

  • Coeff of $x^2 = 0 \implies m^2 = 0 \implies m = 0$.
  • With $m = 0$, the coeff of $x$ becomes $2(0)c - 4a = -4a \neq 0$ (does not vanish).

Hence, no oblique asymptote exists.

Conclusion: The parabola $y^2 = 4ax$ has no asymptotes. Hence proved.

🔥 Repeated in 2017, 2018, 2019 3 Marks

State both geometric and coordinate component formulas along with definitions of $\theta$ and unit normal vector $\hat{n}$.

✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Let $\vec{a} = a_1\hat{i} + a_2\hat{j} + a_3\hat{k}$ and $\vec{b} = b_1\hat{i} + b_2\hat{j} + b_3\hat{k}$, with angle $\theta$ between them ($0 \le \theta \le \pi$).

1. Scalar (Dot) Product $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b}$ (Scalar Quantity):

  • Geometric Formula: $$\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \, |\vec{b}| \cos\theta$$
  • Cartesian Component Form: $$\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = a_1 b_1 + a_2 b_2 + a_3 b_3$$
  • Perpendicular Condition: $\vec{a} \cdot \vec{b} = 0 \iff \vec{a} \perp \vec{b}$ (for non-zero vectors).

  • Geometric Formula: $$\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = |\vec{a}| \, |\vec{b}| \sin\theta \, \hat{n}$$ where $\hat{n}$ is a unit normal vector perpendicular to both $\vec{a}$ and $\vec{b}$ according to the right-hand thumb rule.

  • Parallel Condition: $\vec{a} \times \vec{b} = \vec{0} \iff \vec{a} \parallel \vec{b}$.

Q11. Determine the values of constants $a, b, c$ for which $f(x)$ is continuous at $x = 0$:

🔥 Repeated in 2015, 2017, 2018 7.5 Marks

Given the piecewise function:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} \dfrac{\sin(a+1)x + \sin x}{x}, & x \lt 0 \\[8px] c, & x = 0 \\[8px] \dfrac{(x + b x^2)^{1/2} - x^{1/2}}{b x^{3/2}}, & x \gt 0 \end{cases}$$
✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

For $f(x)$ to be continuous at $x = 0$, we must have:

$$\text{LHL} = \text{RHL} = f(0) = c$$

Step 1: Compute Left Hand Limit ($\text{LHL}$ as $x \to 0^-$):

$$\text{LHL} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{\sin(a+1)x + \sin x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} \left[\frac{\sin(a+1)x}{x} + \frac{\sin x}{x}\right]$$

Using the standard limit $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin k\theta}{\theta} = k$:

$$\text{LHL} = (a + 1) + 1 = a + 2$$

Step 2: Compute Right Hand Limit ($\text{RHL}$ as $x \to 0^+$):

$$\text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{(x + b x^2)^{1/2} - x^{1/2}}{b x^{3/2}} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sqrt{x}\left(\sqrt{1 + bx} - 1\right)}{b x \sqrt{x}} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{\sqrt{1 + bx} - 1}{bx}$$

Rationalize the numerator by multiplying and dividing by $(\sqrt{1 + bx} + 1)$:

$$\text{RHL} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{(1 + bx) - 1}{bx(\sqrt{1 + bx} + 1)} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{bx}{bx(\sqrt{1 + bx} + 1)} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + bx} + 1}$$ $$\text{RHL} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 + 0} + 1} = \frac{1}{2}$$

Step 3: Equate Limits to $f(0)$:

  • From $f(0) = c = \text{RHL} \implies \mathbf{c = \frac{1}{2}}$
  • From $\text{LHL} = c \implies a + 2 = \frac{1}{2} \implies \mathbf{a = -\frac{3}{2}}$
  • Constant $b$ cancels out during simplification, so $\mathbf{b \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \{0\}}$ (any non-zero real number).

Final Answer: $\mathbf{a = -\frac{3}{2}, \quad c = \frac{1}{2}, \quad b \neq 0}$

Q12. Solve the linear system of equations using Cramer's Rule:

🔥 Repeated in 2015 & 2019 7.5 Marks

Apply Cramer's determinant method ($x = D_1/D$, $y = D_2/D$, $z = D_3/D$):

Part (a) · Nov 2019 ($2 \times 2$ System) $$\begin{aligned} 3x + 4y &= 5 \\ x - y &= -3 \end{aligned}$$
Part (b) · Dec 2015 ($3 \times 3$ System) $$\begin{aligned} 6x + y - 3z &= 5 \\ x + 3y - 2z &= 5 \\ 2x + y + 4z &= 8 \end{aligned}$$
✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Part (a) Solution ($2 \times 2$ System · Nov 2019):

1. Coefficient determinant $D$:

$$D = \begin{vmatrix} 3 & 4 \\ 1 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 3(-1) - 4(1) = -3 - 4 = -7 \neq 0$$

2. Determinants $D_1$ and $D_2$:

$$D_1 = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 4 \\ -3 & -1 \end{vmatrix} = 5(-1) - 4(-3) = -5 + 12 = 7$$ $$D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 3 & 5 \\ 1 & -3 \end{vmatrix} = 3(-3) - 5(1) = -9 - 5 = -14$$

3. By Cramer's Rule:

$$x = \frac{D_1}{D} = \frac{7}{-7} = -1, \quad y = \frac{D_2}{D} = \frac{-14}{-7} = 2$$

Result: $\mathbf{x = -1, \quad y = 2}$

Part (b) Solution ($3 \times 3$ System · Dec 2015):

1. Coefficient determinant $D$:

$$D = \begin{vmatrix} 6 & 1 & -3 \\ 1 & 3 & -2 \\ 2 & 1 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 6(12 - (-2)) - 1(4 - (-4)) - 3(1 - 6) = 6(14) - 1(8) - 3(-5) = 84 - 8 + 15 = 91 \neq 0$$

2. Compute $D_1, D_2, D_3$:

$$D_1 = \begin{vmatrix} 5 & 1 & -3 \\ 5 & 3 & -2 \\ 8 & 1 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 5(14) - 1(20+16) - 3(5-24) = 70 - 36 + 57 = 91$$ $$D_2 = \begin{vmatrix} 6 & 5 & -3 \\ 1 & 5 & -2 \\ 2 & 8 & 4 \end{vmatrix} = 6(20+16) - 5(4+4) - 3(8-10) = 216 - 40 + 6 = 182$$ $$D_3 = \begin{vmatrix} 6 & 1 & 5 \\ 1 & 3 & 5 \\ 2 & 1 & 8 \end{vmatrix} = 6(24-5) - 1(8-10) + 5(1-6) = 114 + 2 - 25 = 91$$

3. By Cramer's Rule:

$$x = \frac{D_1}{D} = \frac{91}{91} = 1, \quad y = \frac{D_2}{D} = \frac{182}{91} = 2, \quad z = \frac{D_3}{D} = \frac{91}{91} = 1$$

Result: $\mathbf{x = 1, \quad y = 2, \quad z = 1}$

Matrix $A$ $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 & 5 & 1 \\ 1 & 3 & 4 & 5 \end{bmatrix}$$

Q14. Find the inverse of the matrix $A$ using elementary row transformations:

Dec 2015 7.5 Marks
Matrix $A$ $$A = \begin{bmatrix} 5 & 2 \\ 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

Write the matrix equation $A = I_2 A$:

Q15. Evaluate the partial fractions integral:

🔥 Exact Repeat in 2015 & 2016 7.5 Marks
$$\int \frac{3x + 1}{(x - 1)^2 (x + 3)} \, dx$$
✍️ Step-by-Step Solution:

Step 1: Partial Fraction Decomposition:

$$\frac{3x + 1}{(x - 1)^2 (x + 3)} = \frac{A}{x - 1} + \frac{B}{(x - 1)^2} + \frac{C}{x + 3}$$

Multiplying both sides by $(x - 1)^2 (x + 3)$:

$$3x + 1 = A(x - 1)(x + 3) + B(x + 3) + C(x - 1)^2$$

Part (a) · Dec 2015 Examination Problem

If $y = e^{m \cos^{-1} x}$, show that:

$$(1 - x^2) y_{n+2} - (2n + 1)x y_{n+1} - (n^2 + m^2) y_n = 0$$

and determine the exact value of $y_n(0)$.

✍️ Full 15-Mark Solution for Part (a):

Statement of Leibniz’s Theorem: If $u$ and $v$ are functions of $x$ possessing derivatives of order $n$, then:

$$(u \cdot v)_n = u_n v + \binom{n}{1} u_{n-1} v_1 + \binom{n}{2} u_{n-2} v_2 + \dots + \binom{n}{r} u_{n-r} v_r + \dots + u v_n$$

Step 1: Differentiate the given function:

Given $y = e^{m \cos^{-1} x}$. Differentiating with respect to $x$ using chain rule:

$$y_1 = \frac{dy}{dx} = e^{m \cos^{-1} x} \cdot \frac{d}{dx}(m \cos^{-1} x) = e^{m \cos^{-1} x} \cdot \left(\frac{-m}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}\right)$$

Since $y = e^{m \cos^{-1} x}$, substitute $y$ back:

$$y_1 = \frac{-m y}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$$

Step 2: Cross-multiply and square both sides:

$$\sqrt{1 - x^2} \, y_1 = -m y$$ $$(1 - x^2) y_1^2 = m^2 y^2$$

Step 3: Differentiate again to establish the base 2nd-order equation:

$$\frac{d}{dx}\left[(1 - x^2) y_1^2\right] = \frac{d}{dx}\left[m^2 y^2\right]$$ $$(1 - x^2) \cdot (2 y_1 y_2) + y_1^2 \cdot (-2x) = m^2 (2 y y_1)$$ $$2 y_1 \left[(1 - x^2) y_2 - x y_1\right] = 2 y_1 (m^2 y)$$

Dividing throughout by $2y_1$ (since $y_1 \neq 0$):

$$(1 - x^2) y_2 - x y_1 - m^2 y = 0 \quad \text{--- (Equation 1)}$$

Step 4: Differentiate Equation (1) $n$ times using Leibniz’s Theorem:

Applying Leibniz’s formula term by term:

  • Term 1: $[(1 - x^2) y_2]_n$ (with $u = y_2, v = 1 - x^2$): $$= (y_2)_n (1 - x^2) + \binom{n}{1} (y_2)_{n-1} \frac{d}{dx}(1 - x^2) + \binom{n}{2} (y_2)_{n-2} \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(1 - x^2) + 0$$ $$= y_{n+2}(1 - x^2) + n y_{n+1}(-2x) + \frac{n(n-1)}{2} y_n(-2)$$ $$= (1 - x^2) y_{n+2} - 2n x y_{n+1} - n(n-1) y_n$$
  • Term 2: $-[x y_1]_n$ (with $u = y_1, v = x$): $$= -[(y_1)_n x + \binom{n}{1} (y_1)_{n-1} (1) + 0] = -[y_{n+1} x + n y_n] = -x y_{n+1} - n y_n$$
  • Term 3: $-[m^2 y]_n$: $$= -m^2 y_n$$

Adding all three expanded terms:

$$(1 - x^2) y_{n+2} - 2n x y_{n+1} - n(n-1) y_n - x y_{n+1} - n y_n - m^2 y_n = 0$$ $$(1 - x^2) y_{n+2} - (2n + 1)x y_{n+1} - [n(n-1) + n + m^2] y_n = 0$$

Notice that $n(n-1) + n = n^2 - n + n = n^2$. Therefore:

$$\mathbf{(1 - x^2) y_{n+2} - (2n + 1)x y_{n+1} - (n^2 + m^2) y_n = 0} \quad \text{--- (Equation 2, Hence Proved)}$$

Step 5: Determination of $y_n(0)$:

Substitute $x = 0$ in Equation (2):

$$(1 - 0) y_{n+2}(0) - (2n + 1)(0) y_{n+1}(0) - (n^2 + m^2) y_n(0) = 0$$ $$\mathbf{y_{n+2}(0) = (n^2 + m^2) y_n(0)} \quad \text{--- (Recurrence Relation)}$$

Now evaluate base values at $x = 0$:

  • $y(0) = e^{m \cos^{-1}(0)} = e^{m \pi/2}$
  • $y_1(0) = \frac{-m y(0)}{\sqrt{1 - 0}} = -m e^{m \pi/2}$
  • From Equation (1) at $x = 0$: $y_2(0) = m^2 y(0) = m^2 e^{m \pi/2}$

Using the recurrence relation $y_{n+2}(0) = (n^2 + m^2) y_n(0)$:

  • For odd values of $n$:
    • Put $n = 1 \implies y_3(0) = (1^2 + m^2) y_1(0) = -(1^2 + m^2) m e^{m \pi/2}$
    • Put $n = 3 \implies y_5(0) = (3^2 + m^2) y_3(0) = -(3^2 + m^2)(1^2 + m^2) m e^{m \pi/2}$
    $$\mathbf{y_n(0) = -m (1^2 + m^2)(3^2 + m^2) \dots ((n-2)^2 + m^2) e^{m \pi/2} \quad \text{(when } n \text{ is odd)}}$$
  • For even values of $n$:
    • Put $n = 2 \implies y_4(0) = (2^2 + m^2) y_2(0) = (2^2 + m^2) m^2 e^{m \pi/2}$
    • Put $n = 4 \implies y_6(0) = (4^2 + m^2) y_4(0) = (4^2 + m^2)(2^2 + m^2) m^2 e^{m \pi/2}$
    $$\mathbf{y_n(0) = m^2 (2^2 + m^2)(4^2 + m^2) \dots ((n-2)^2 + m^2) e^{m \pi/2} \quad \text{(when } n \text{ is even)}}$$
